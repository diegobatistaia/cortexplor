Jensen Huang Apologizes to Nvidia Staff, Denies Trump Pressure

In his first all-hands since returning as CEO, Huang admitted violating Nvidia's code of conduct and pushed back on claims that Trump drove his reinstatement, according to Wired and the Guardian.

Newsroom Cortexplor Checked against 4 sources

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang apologized to employees on Tuesday during his first all-hands meeting since returning to the role, and admitted he violated the company's code of conduct over extramarital affairs, according to Wired and the Guardian.

Huang resigned on Nov. 12 after reports surfaced about the affairs, including one with a woman U.S. intelligence suspects has ties to Chinese intelligence. Nvidia's board reinstated him on Nov. 23, as <a href="https://cortexplor.com/en/nvidia-reinstates-jensen-huang-as-ceo">Cortexplor reported</a> at the time.

What Huang told staff

"I made some terrible mistakes. I was unfaithful to Lori, and I hurt my kids and, of course, I hurt all of you," Huang said, according to a livestream of the meeting reviewed by the New York Times and cited by the Guardian.

CNBC, which spoke to people inside the room, confirms the closing line of that remark: Huang told employees he had hurt all of them. Wired, which reviewed remarks from the meeting, reports that Huang acknowledged breaking internal policy and called his conduct "very serious personal mistakes" that damaged his relationship with his wife and two children.

A difficult meeting, and ongoing therapy

Huang described the meeting as the second hardest of his life, behind only a conversation with his wife, according to both Wired and CNBC. "This is going to be the hardest company meeting I've ever done, and the second hardest meeting I have ever done. The hardest meeting I've ever done was with my wife," he said, per Wired.

Wired reports Huang told staff he is in therapy and that the sessions have been helping him. CNBC, citing people present, confirms he raised his therapy sessions during the meeting.

Huang denies Trump pressured the board

Huang pushed back on reports that President Donald Trump was behind his reinstatement. The Guardian reports he called that narrative "completely untrue" and said the board acted after it "evaluated the entire situation" and "gathered facts." Wired reports the same denial, with Huang saying the decision belonged to the board, not "the highest levels of our government."

Succession planning in question

Huang also addressed succession planning during the meeting. Wired reports he called the formal process of naming a successor "flawed." CNBC, citing people present, reports Huang said Nvidia doesn't need a traditional succession plan because the company already has multiple capable leaders running different parts of the business.

Culture risk inside the company

TechCrunch notes that a code-of-conduct breach like Huang's would typically cost any other Nvidia employee their job. Compliance experts interviewed by the outlet say the bigger risk for Huang isn't legal but cultural: employees may feel embarrassed by their leadership's conduct even without formal punishment.

TechCrunch reports that Matt Plass, a compliance expert who recently surveyed thousands of workers on corporate codes of conduct, said companies that remove an executive over similar violations typically follow up with a campaign to reassure staff, investors and customers that the organization has changed. Plass told the outlet that approach is harder to apply at a company like Nvidia, where Huang is widely seen as central to its success and the board ultimately chose to bring him back rather than replace him.
